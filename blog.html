1. A marketing funnel is a model—not a guaranteed path

A funnel groups a simplified journey into stages such as awareness, consideration and action. It helps a team ask what a person may need at each stage. Real journeys are rarely perfectly linear: people return to search, ask friends, compare products on different devices or decide not to buy.

Try this: for a local bakery, list one useful piece of information for a person discovering the business, one for someone comparing breakfast boxes and one for someone ready to order. Do not assume every person moves through all three stages.

2. A metric needs a definition before it needs a dashboard

“Conversion rate” is incomplete until you state what action counts, which group forms the denominator and which dates are included. For example: 18 orders from 60 enquiries = 18 ÷ 60 × 100 = 30% enquiry-to-order rate for that example. It is not the same as orders divided by website visits.

Try this: write the numerator and denominator next to every percentage in your next report.

3. A useful social content calendar starts with audience questions

Before writing captions, collect the questions people actually ask. Group them into repeatable themes such as product details, how-to guidance, behind-the-scenes proof and customer support. Then choose formats and a publishing rhythm the team can maintain.

Try this: write three questions from one audience. Turn each into one post idea, then add a clear next step. Avoid making every post a promotion.

4. More traffic cannot fix a broken customer journey

If a page has unclear pricing, a broken form or missing collection details, buying more visits may simply send more people into the same friction. Check the landing page and the full task before increasing a campaign budget.

Try this: ask a friend to find a price and complete a sample enquiry on a phone. Watch where they pause without coaching them.
